Robotic vision

A camera gives the robot a photo, not the answers. Like a detective, it must infer where it is, what is around it and what to do from clues in the pixels.

An image is a spreadsheet

A greyscale image is a grid of numbers (0 = black, 255 = white for uint8). Colour is three stacked grids: red, green, blue.

In the practical: you index pixels as img[v, u]: row first, then column.

Convolution

A small stencil slides over the grid; at each spot you multiply-and-add. Different stencils blur, sharpen or find edges. CNNs learn the stencils.

In the practical: the filtering cells apply blur and edge kernels.

Edges are slopes

An edge is where brightness changes fast, like a cliff on a hill map. The derivative measures the slope, and the gradient arrow points uphill.

1. What robotic vision is

Definition slides p.10: robotic vision is the use of cameras and algorithms to estimate information about the world that a robot needs for actions. The comparison table slides p.12 is worth memorising:

FieldInput → OutputExample
Image processingimage → imageblur, sharpen

Robot learningimage + actions + data → behavioursRL, imitation learning

The three big questions slides p.13: Where am I? (geometry, features, VO, SLAM: Weeks 2–7), What is around me? (detection, segmentation, representations: Weeks 8–12), When does vision fail? (occlusion, lighting, ambiguity, uncertainty, distribution shift: Week 13).

All examples share three things slides p.30 slides p.31 slides p.33: they start from images, not the true state (motion, location, 3D, identity, grasp and action must be inferred); they output a task-specific representation (trajectory, map, boxes, grasp, policy); and they face ambiguity. VO can be fooled by a moving object, SLAM by two similar-looking places, a detector by a novel object, a grasp planner by occlusion, a policy by out-of-distribution situations.

Exam angle. Scenario questions often begin “what must the robot infer, and what could make it confidently wrong?”. Answer with the p33 failure list. The lecturer’s advice slides p.37: for any method ask what problem it solves, what it estimates, why the robot needs it, what assumptions make it work and when it fails.

2. Images are arrays

Colour images have three channels (R, G, B); greyscale has one slides p.51. Datatype matters slides p.53: uint8 holds 0–255 and wraps around on overflow; float32 is usually 0–1. Pixel access is img[v, u], i.e. [row, column], with the origin at the top-left, u to the right and v downwards slides p.59. u, v are used because x, y, z already name 3D coordinates.

Worked example. An image is 480 rows × 640 columns. The pixel at u = 100, v = 20 is img[20, 100]. img[100, 20] would be row 100, column 20, a different pixel.

3. Thresholding and chromaticity

Thresholding keeps pixels brighter or darker than a value slides p.62. Testing “very red” with R > 150 also fires on white pixels, because white has a high R too slides p.65. The fix is chromaticity: \(r = R/(R+G+B)\), which measures the proportion of red independent of brightness slides p.66.

Worked example. Red pixel (200, 50, 50): \(r = 200/300 = 0.667\). White pixel (250, 250, 250): \(r = 250/750 = 0.333\). The threshold \(r > 0.5\) keeps the red pixel and rejects the white one, which a raw R > 150 test cannot do.

4. Neighbourhood operations and convolution

Replace each pixel by a value computed from its neighbours slides p.67. Convolution slides a kernel over the image and sums kernel × patch slides p.69. Traditional kernels are hand-designed (blur, sharpen, edge); in a CNN the kernels are learned from data slides p.72. This is the same operation, which is why Week 1 links to Week 8.

5. Edges and image derivatives

An edge is where intensity changes rapidly slides p.73. For a 1-D function the central finite difference is \(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x} \approx \dfrac{f(x+\epsilon) - f(x-\epsilon)}{2\epsilon}\) slides p.76. With \(f(x)=x^2\), \(x=2\), \(\epsilon=1\): \((9-1)/2 = 4\), matching the true derivative \(2x = 4\).

Images have two partial derivatives slides p.78: \(I_x \approx \tfrac{I(x+1,y) - I(x-1,y)}{2}\), \(I_y \approx \tfrac{I(x,y+1) - I(x,y-1)}{2}\). Each is a convolution with \([-\tfrac12, 0, \tfrac12]\) slides p.79.

The gradient \(\nabla I = (I_x, I_y)\) points in the direction of steepest ascent. Its direction \(\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(I_y, I_x)\) is perpendicular to the edge, and its magnitude \(\|\nabla I\| = \sqrt{I_x^2 + I_y^2}\) is the edge strength slides p.80.

Worked example. Row of pixels [10, 20, 60, 100, 110]. At the third pixel (value 60): \(I_x = (100-20)/2 = 40\). At the fourth: \((110-60)/2 = 25\). The edge is strongest around the third pixel. If somewhere \(I_x = 30, I_y = 40\), then \(\|\nabla I\| = 50\) and \(\theta = 53.1^\circ\).

Noise makes raw derivatives spiky slides p.81, so smooth first and look for peaks of \(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}(I * H)\) slides p.82. Because differentiation commutes with convolution, \(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}(I*H) = I * \tfrac{\partial H}{\partial x}\): convolve once with a derivative-of-Gaussian kernel slides p.83.

Exam angle. Expect a two-line central-difference calculation, “which direction does the gradient point?” (across the edge), or “why smooth before differentiating?” (noise amplification).

Explain it like I’m five

Detector = “where to look”

Pick spots you could find again, such as the corner of a window, not the middle of a blank wall. Good spots are distinctive, repeatable and well localised.

In the practical: SuperPoint picks the keypoints that LightGlue then matches on KITTI.

Descriptor = fingerprint

Describe the neighbourhood of each spot as a list of numbers (SIFT: 128 floats) or bits (BRIEF/ORB: 256 bits), so the same spot in another photo gets a similar fingerprint.

Matching = speed dating

Each point in image A looks for its most similar partner in B. If the best and second-best partners are almost equally good, walk away (ratio test). Only keep pairs who choose each other (cross-check).

Learned matching

SuperGlue lets all the points “talk” within and across both images before deciding, like solving a jigsaw by looking at neighbouring pieces. Points with no partner go in the dustbin.

In the practical: LightGlue (SuperGlue’s faster successor) stops early on easy frame pairs.

1. Why correspondences, and why they are hard

The VO pipeline slides p.12 has two stages: (1) visual correspondences (find distinctive regions and the same region in the next image), then (2) motion estimation from them (Weeks 4–5). “Odometry” comes from the Greek odos (route) and metron (measure) slides p.6.

The challenges slides p.18 slides p.19 slides p.20: geometric (scale change, rotation, perspective distortion), appearance (light and shadow, season and weather, exposure and white balance, blur and noise) and ambiguity (repeated patterns, visual aliasing, low texture). Repeated floor tiles give several plausible matches for one feature (practice Q2).

The general pipeline is detection → description → matching slides p.21. Some methods cover two stages (SIFT = detector + descriptor) and some are combinations (ORB = FAST + BRIEF) slides p.22.

2. What makes a good feature

A good feature is distinctive (different enough from other regions to match unambiguously), repeatable (detected again under small changes of viewpoint, scale, illumination and noise) and well localised (a sharp position, so small image changes don’t shift it much) slides p.36. A corner beats an edge, because along an edge you can slide without change (the aperture problem), and an edge beats a flat patch.

3. Moravec and Harris corners

Moravec (1980) shifts a small window in a few directions and measures the change with SSD:

\[s(u,v,\delta) = \sum_{i,j}\big(I(u+\delta_u+i,\ v+\delta_v+j) - I(u+i,\ v+j)\big)^2\]

The score is the minimum over shifts, so a corner must change in every direction slides p.46. Weaknesses: it gives high scores on edges that are not at 0°, 45° or 90° (only 8 directions are tested), and it is inefficient.

Harris (1988) uses image derivatives instead. The structure tensor is \(A = w * \begin{bmatrix} J_u^2 & J_uJ_v \\ J_uJ_v & J_v^2\end{bmatrix}\), with \(w\) a window or Gaussian weighting slides p.48. Its eigenvectors give the directions of fastest and slowest change; its eigenvalues give how much change slides p.49:

  • both eigenvalues large → corner
  • one large, one small → edge
  • both small → flat slides p.50
Worked example. \(A = \mathrm{diag}(50, 48)\) → corner. \(A = \mathrm{diag}(50, 1)\) → edge (strong change in one direction only). \(A = \mathrm{diag}(1, 0.5)\) → flat.

4. Descriptors: SIFT and BRIEF

Comparing raw patches with SSD \(\sum(a-b)^2\) or SAD \(\sum|a-b|\) is naive: it breaks under rotation, scale and illumination change slides p.57.

SIFT (1999/2004) aims to be invariant to scale, rotation, moderate perspective, illumination and noise slides p.60:

  1. Scale space: blur with increasing σ (like seeing the scene from further away) and take the Difference of Gaussians \(D(u,v,\sigma) = L(u,v,k\sigma) - L(u,v,\sigma)\) slides p.62.
  2. Extrema: a candidate must be a max or min compared with its neighbours in the same scale and the scales above and below (26 neighbours) slides p.63.
  3. Orientation: histogram of gradient orientations weighted by magnitude; the strongest bin is the keypoint orientation, giving rotation invariance slides p.64.
  4. Clean-up: remove edge-like, low-contrast and badly localised candidates slides p.65.
  5. Descriptor: 4×4 sub-regions, an 8-bin orientation histogram each, concatenated: 4·4·8 = 128 numbers slides p.66.

BRIEF (2010) compares random pairs of pixels in a smoothed patch; each comparison gives one bit (e.g. 256 bits = 32 bytes) slides p.70. Matching uses the Hamming distance (XOR then count bits), which is very fast and needs no floating point slides p.71. It is not rotation or scale invariant and needs a separate detector slides p.72.

Worked example. Hamming distance between 10110010 and 10011010: XOR = 00101000 → 2 bits differ.

5. FAST and ORB

FAST (2006) looks at a circle of 16 pixels around candidate p. If N contiguous pixels (typically N ≥ 12) are all brighter than \(I_p + t\) or all darker than \(I_p - t\), p is a corner. A high-speed test checks pixels 1, 9, 5 and 13 first; for a corner at least 3 of them must pass slides p.76. A decision tree learned from data picks the test order to minimise comparisons slides p.77. There are no gradients, no convolution and no eigenvalues slides p.78.

ORB (2011) = oFAST + rBRIEF slides p.83 slides p.84. oFAST adds a scale pyramid (an idea from SIFT), a Harris score to reject points on lines, and an orientation from the vector between the keypoint and the intensity centroid of its patch. rBRIEF rotates BRIEF’s sampling pattern by that orientation.

6. Matching: brute force, ratio test, cross-check

Brute force: for each descriptor in A, find the most similar in B, using Euclidean distance for float descriptors and Hamming for binary ones slides p.87.

Lowe’s ratio test slides p.88: accept if \(\dfrac{\|A - B_1\|}{\|A - B_2\|} < \theta\) (best over second best). A ratio near 1 means the match is ambiguous.

Cross-check (mutual NN) slides p.89: discard \((A_1, B_1)\) if some \(A_2\) is closer to \(B_1\) than \(A_1\) is.

Worked example (practice Q4). Distances 35 and 40, θ = 0.80, strict “<”. Ratio = 35/40 = 0.875, which is not below 0.80 → reject. With distances 24 and 40 the ratio is 0.6 → accept.

Practical baseline slides p.103: ORB + Hamming brute force + ratio test or cross-check, then RANSAC geometric verification when false matches remain (Week 4). Try SIFT for large scale or viewpoint change and weak texture, or when you need fewer but more reliable matches (at higher cost) slides p.102. Use approximate NN for large descriptor sets.

Tutorial paper · in 60 seconds

SuperGlue (CVPR 2020) and LightGlue (ICCV 2023)

Problem. Nearest-neighbour matching plus heuristics (ratio test, mutual check) fails with repeated structure, large viewpoint change and occlusion. SuperGlue is a learned “middle-end” between the front end (feature extraction) and the back end (pose / MAP estimation).

Key idea. The inputs are two sets of local features (keypoint position + confidence, and a descriptor). A keypoint encoder adds position to each descriptor. An attentional graph neural network alternates self-attention (within an image: context) and cross-attention (between images: candidate matches) for 9 layers. It then builds a score matrix with an extra dustbin row and column for unmatched points and solves a differentiable optimal-transport problem (Sinkhorn, 100 iterations). The result is a partial assignment in which each point gets at most one match paper p.5.

Headline numbers. Indoor ScanNet with SuperPoint features (Table 2 paper p.6): pose AUC@20° rises from 36.40 (NN + mutual) to 51.84, and match precision from 50.4% to 84.4%. 12M parameters, 69 ms per pair (15 FPS) on a GTX 1080.

LightGlue keeps SuperGlue’s accuracy but adapts its compute. It stops early once its predictions are confident (adaptive depth) and prunes points it judges unmatchable (adaptive width). On MegaDepth (Table 2): SuperGlue AUC@5/10/20 = 49.7/67.1/80.6 at 70.0 ms; LightGlue 49.9/67.0/80.1 at 44.2 ms; adaptive LightGlue 49.4/67.2/80.1 at 31.4 ms.

Limitation. Both are sparse and depend on the external detector: no keypoints means nothing to match. Attention cost grows with the number of keypoints.

How to read their results. “AUC@10°” is the area under the cumulative pose-error curve up to 10°: higher means more image pairs have accurate relative pose. Precision is the fraction of predicted matches that are correct; matching score is correct matches over all detected keypoints. In an ablation table, compare each “No X” row with the full model: the biggest drop marks the most important component.

Tutorial: full discussion (optional depth)

SuperGlue

1. What problem? Independent descriptor matching ignores context: a point on a repeated window has several equally good partners, and fixed heuristics either keep wrong matches or throw away good ones. Matching is a joint assignment: one physical point can match at most one point, and the choices must be geometrically consistent with each other. Some points (occluded, out of view, unrepeatable) have no partner, so the method must also decide what stays unmatched (dustbin).

2. Inputs. For each image: keypoint positions with detection confidence, plus local descriptors (SuperPoint or SIFT). SuperGlue adds positional encoding and context through attention. Detection and description happen before it, and pose estimation (RANSAC + E/PnP) after.

3. Ingredients. Self-attention (same image): each feature learns about salient points around it. Cross-attention (other image): it looks for candidate matches, like a human glancing back and forth. Optimal transport / Sinkhorn: jointly selects one-to-one correspondences. Dustbin: absorbs features with no reliable match. A broader view helps an ambiguous point: if its neighbours match consistently, the correct candidate is the one that fits that pattern.

4. Capabilities and limits. It improves most with wide baselines, repeated structure and large appearance change. The authors evaluate by downstream pose accuracy (AUC), precision and matching score, not just match counts. Limits: GPU cost, quadratic attention in the number of keypoints, and reliance on the detector (poor or few keypoints means poor matches).

LightGlue

1. What remains? It keeps SuperGlue’s attention-based joint reasoning and dustbin idea, but SuperGlue is costly in time and memory and hard to train. Robots need low latency and fit on embedded compute: VO runs at camera rate and other modules share the processor.

2. Central idea. Easy pairs (large overlap, little appearance change) become confident after a few layers, so the network checks its confidence after each layer and exits early (depth). Points that are clearly unmatchable are dropped from later layers (width). Table 5: easy pairs stop at layer 4.7 on average (hard ones 6.9) with a 1.86× speed-up.

3. Comparison. It is not only faster: accuracy matches or slightly exceeds SuperGlue (Table 2), and it trains in a few GPU-days. Adaptive compute lowers average latency while still spending more on hard pairs, which suits real-time and embedded robots.

4. Limits. It still needs an external detector and descriptor. With too few keypoints, matching fails. It is a sparse matcher. Detector-free dense matchers (LoFTR) are preferable in low texture, under extreme viewpoint change, or when dense correspondence is needed, at a higher compute cost (LoFTR 181 ms vs 44 ms).

Ablation from SuperGlue Table 4 paper p.7 (AUC@20 / precision): NN + mutual 36.40/50.4 · no GNN 38.56/66.0 · no cross-attention 42.57/74.0 · no positional encoding 47.12/75.8 · 3 layers 46.93/79.9 · full (9 layers) 51.84/84.4.

The pinhole

A dark box with a tiny hole draws an upside-down picture of the world on the back wall. Far things look small because their rays come in at a shallow angle: x = f·X/Z.

Intrinsics K

K is the camera’s personal ruler: focal length in pixels and where the optical axis hits the sensor. It converts metres on the image plane into pixel numbers.

In the practical: you print KITTI’s K and see f ≈ 7×10² px with the principal point near the image centre.

Extrinsics R, t

Where the camera stands and which way it faces. Same camera, different pose, different photo.

Stereo = your two eyes

Close one eye, then the other: near things jump more than far things. That jump is disparity; depth = f·baseline / disparity.

In the practical: StereoSGBM computes this jump for every pixel along each row.

Baseline trade-off

Eyes further apart judge far distances better but struggle with things right in front of the nose.

1. The pinhole camera and central projection

Similar triangles give \(x/f = X/Z\), so \(x = fX/Z,\ y = fY/Z\) slides p.17. In homogeneous coordinates slides p.21 slides p.22:

\[\begin{bmatrix}\tilde x\\ \tilde y\\ \tilde z\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} f&0&0&0\\0&f&0&0\\0&0&1&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\\1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}fX\\fY\\Z\end{bmatrix} \Rightarrow (x,y) = (fX/Z,\ fY/Z)\]

To get a Cartesian point, divide by the third element.

Pixels slides p.25: \(u = x/\rho_u + u_0\), \(v = y/\rho_v + v_0\), where ρ is the pixel size and \((u_0,v_0)\) the principal point, with the origin at the top-left. Together these give the intrinsic matrix \(K = \begin{bmatrix} f/\rho_u & 0 & u_0\\ 0 & f/\rho_v & v_0 \\ 0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\) (focal length in pixels).

Complete model slides p.27: \(\tilde{\mathbf p} = K\,[\,\text{projection}\,]\,\begin{bmatrix}R&t\\0&1\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\mathbf P\). The inverse appears because \((R,t)\) is the camera’s pose in the world and we need world → camera. The 3×4 camera matrix \(C\) is intrinsics × extrinsics slides p.28. Scaling \(C\) by any λ gives the same pixel (scale invariance), so \(C_{34}\) can be fixed to 1, leaving 11 unknowns slides p.29 slides p.30.

Worked example. f = 5 mm, ρ = 20 µm → f = 250 px; principal point (320, 240). The camera-frame point (0.4, −0.2, 2.0) m projects to \(u = 320 + 250\cdot0.4/2 = 370\), \(v = 240 + 250\cdot(-0.2)/2 = 215\).

Practice Q10 runs the other way (back-projection): \(X = (u - c_u)Z/f = (270-320)\cdot4/500 = -0.40\) m.

2. What projection does to the scene

  • No unique inverse: depth is lost, and infinitely many 3D worlds give the same image slides p.32.
  • Lengths and angles are not preserved; parallelism and perpendicularity are lost; straight lines stay straight slides p.33.
  • Parallel lines meet at a vanishing point (possibly outside the image); parallel planes meet in a vanishing line slides p.34.

3. Sensor and lens details

Bayer pattern slides p.35: RGGB filters with twice as many green, because luminance depends mostly on green and human vision is more sensitive to luminance detail. Missing colours are interpolated (demosaicing).

Global vs rolling shutter slides p.36: global exposes all pixels at once (no distortion of moving objects, more costly); rolling reads rows one after another (cheap, but skews moving objects, which breaks the pinhole two-view model in Week 4).

Aperture slides p.40: a large aperture gives blur; a pinhole lets in little light, which needs long exposure and causes motion blur; too small an aperture causes diffraction. Hence lenses. Thin lens equation: \(\frac1f = \frac1Z + \frac1e\) slides p.45. Points not in focus spread into a blur circle \(R = L\delta/(2e)\) slides p.47 (keep R < 1 pixel). When \(Z \gg f\) and \(Z \gg L\), \(e \approx f\): the pinhole approximation slides p.50 slides p.51.

Worked example. f = 50 mm, object at Z = 2 m: \(e = 1/(1/50 - 1/2000) = 51.28\) mm. The image plane sits only 1.3 mm behind the focal point, which is why the pinhole model works.

4. Stereo: disparity, triangulation and baseline

With two identical, aligned cameras, points in the right image shift left relative to the left image, and distant points shift less slides p.57. Disparity \(d = u_L - u_R \propto fb/Z\) slides p.58. It is found by block matching along the same row slides p.60. Triangulation slides p.63:

\[Z = \frac{f\,b}{u_L - u_R},\qquad X = \frac{Z\,u_L}{f},\qquad Y = \frac{Z\,v_L}{f}\quad(u,v\text{ relative to the principal point, }f\text{ in pixels})\]

Depth error grows with the square of depth: \(\delta Z \approx \frac{Z^2}{fb}\,\delta d\). Example: f = 700 px, b = 0.12 m, Z = 5 m, δd = 0.5 px gives δZ ≈ 0.149 m.

5. Calibration and homographies

For points on a plane (Z = 0), the 3×4 camera matrix collapses to a 3×3 homography H with 8 unknowns (scale is arbitrary). Each point gives 2 equations, so 4 points determine H slides p.72. The DLT stacks the equations as \(Q\mathbf h = 0\) and solves with SVD slides p.89. Zhang’s method uses several views of a checkerboard to get K through \(B = K^{-\top}K^{-1}\) and a Cholesky decomposition; the lecturer marks those details as not examined slides p.92. Lens distortion is radial: barrel, pincushion or fisheye slides p.94.

1. Epipolar geometry

Two cameras (or one moving camera) view a static scene, with unknown R, t and even unknown K slides p.14. A pixel x in image 1 back-projects to a ray. That ray projects into image 2 as a line l′, and the match x′ must lie on it. This holds whatever the point’s depth slides p.23 slides p.25.

  • Epipolar plane: the plane through X, C and C′. Epipolar lines: where that plane cuts the images slides p.27.
  • Epipole: the image of the other camera’s centre, which is where the baseline meets the image plane. All epipolar lines pass through it.
  • Ideal (rectified) stereo slides p.28: epipolar lines are horizontal and parallel and the epipoles are at infinity, so the correspondence search is a 1-D search along the row.

2. The fundamental matrix

The epipolar line of x is \(l' = Fx\), and x′ on l′ means \(x'^\top l' = 0\), so \(x'^\top F x = 0\) for every correct correspondence slides p.32. F is 3×3 and rank 2, with 7 degrees of freedom.

8-point algorithm slides p.33 slides p.34: each correspondence gives one linear equation in the 9 entries of F, \(A\mathbf f = 0\). Fix \(\|\mathbf f\|=1\) (scale), take the SVD of A, use the last row of \(V^\top\), reshape it to 3×3, force rank 2 with a second SVD (zero the smallest singular value), and denormalise: \(F = T'^\top\tilde F T\) (Hartley normalisation). The OpenCV function is findFundamentalMat; slide p33 says findHomography, which is a slip.

Worked example. Rectified pair, pure x-translation: \(F = \begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\0&0&-1\\0&1&0\end{bmatrix}\). For x = (100, 50, 1), \(l' = Fx = (0, -1, 50)\), i.e. the line \(-y + 50 = 0\), so y = 50, the same row. The candidate x′ = (80, 50, 1) gives \(x'^\top l' = -50 + 50 = 0\) ✓.

When F fails slides p.35. True degeneracies: pure rotation (no translation, so no epipoles), a planar scene (a homography explains the data and F is not unique), collinear or clustered points. Near-degenerate (poorly conditioned): very small baseline, distant points (weak parallax), forward motion near the epipole, dynamic scenes, lens distortion or rolling shutter. Practical checks slides p.36: inlier count, spatial spread, H-vs-F comparison, Sampson error, cheirality, resampling stability, temporal consistency. OpenCV returns a matrix even for degenerate data.

3. RANSAC

You need correct matches to estimate F, but you need F to find the correct matches. RANSAC breaks the loop slides p.42 slides p.43:

  1. fit the model to a random minimal sample (8 pairs for F, 5 for E, 2 points for a line);
  2. compute every data point’s error against that model;
  3. points with error < threshold are inliers;
  4. repeat k times;
  5. keep the model with the largest inlier set (consensus) slides p.54.

It works because some random minimal samples contain only inliers, and those produce models supported by many points (practice Q12).

How many iterations? slides p.55 slides p.56 With inlier ratio w, sample size n and desired success probability p:

\[k = \frac{\log(1-p)}{\log(1-w^n)}\]
Worked example. w = 0.9, n = 8, p = 0.999 → \(w^8 = 0.430\), k = 12.3 → 13 (slide p56). w = 0.75 → k = 65.5 (the slide says 65; rounding up gives 66). With w = 0.5, p = 0.99: n = 8 → 1177, n = 5 → 146, n = 3 → 35. The all-inlier probability \(w^n\) shrinks exponentially with n, which is why minimal solvers matter (practice Q21).

The threshold (practice Q13): with the model and residuals fixed, raising the threshold from 2 to 5 px keeps every old inlier and may turn some outliers into inliers. A looser threshold admits more noise, so refinement does not necessarily get better.

Variants slides p.57: PROSAC (sample high-ranked matches first), LO-RANSAC (locally re-fit promising models), preemptive verification, GC-RANSAC (spatially coherent labels), MAGSAC++ (marginalise over noise scales, so it is less threshold-sensitive), USAC (a modular framework).

4. The essential matrix and motion from 2D–2D

With K known, normalise the points: \(\hat x = K^{-1}x\). Then \(\hat x'^\top E\,\hat x = 0\) slides p.62, with \(E = K'^\top F K\) slides p.64. E can be estimated from ≥ 5 points (findEssentialMat) slides p.63. For cameras \([I|0]\) and \([R|t]\), \(E = [t]_\times R\), where \([t]_\times = \begin{bmatrix}0&-t_z&t_y\\t_z&0&-t_x\\-t_y&t_x&0\end{bmatrix}\) slides p.66. E has 5 DOF: 3 for rotation and 2 for the translation direction.

Decomposition gives 4 solutions slides p.68: \([R_1,t],[R_1,-t],[R_2,t],[R_2,-t]\), where \(R_1, R_2\) differ by a 180° twist about the baseline. Triangulate the points; only one solution puts them in front of both cameras (positive depth, cheirality). recoverPose does this in one go slides p.69.

Scale ambiguity slides p.72: R is correct, but t is known only up to scale (direction, not distance). Scaling the scene and the translation together leaves every image identical (Week 5 shows the algebra).

Tutorial paper · in 60 seconds

Visual Odometry, Part I (Scaramuzza & Fraundorfer, IEEE RAM 2011)

Problem. VO estimates a vehicle’s egomotion from cameras alone, incrementally, like wheel odometry but immune to wheel slip. It needs enough light, a static textured scene and frame overlap. Typical relative position error is 0.1–2% paper p.1.

Key formulation paper p.5: \(T_k = \begin{bmatrix}R_{k,k-1}&t_{k,k-1}\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) is the relative motion between frames k−1 and k. \(C_k\) is the pose relative to frame 0, with \(C_n = C_{n-1}T_n\). Feature-based methods are more accurate and cheaper than appearance-based ones.

Algorithm 1 (2D–2D) paper p.9: capture \(I_k\) → extract and match features → compute E → decompose into \(R_k, t_k\) → compute the relative scale and rescale \(t_k\) → \(C_k = C_{k-1}T_k\) → repeat. Relative scale \(r = \frac{\|X_{k-1,i} - X_{k-1,j}\|}{\|X_{k,i} - X_{k,j}\|}\) from triangulated point pairs (median for robustness); it needs features tracked over at least 3 frames.

Limitation. Errors in every \(T_k\) are chained, so the path drifts. Windowed bundle adjustment reduced the final error by a factor of 2–5 over 10 km paper p.4. VO keeps only local consistency; SLAM aims for global consistency.

Tutorial: full discussion (optional depth)

1. Relative motion vs pose. \(T_k\) is the rigid transform between consecutive frames (k−1 → k); \(C_k\) is the camera pose relative to the first frame, \(C_k = C_{k-1}T_k = C_0T_1T_2\cdots T_k\). Every \(T_k\) carries a small error, and later poses inherit all earlier errors. A small rotation error also rotates every later translation, so position error grows faster than linearly with distance: drift.

2. From correspondences to motion. Each calibrated correspondence gives one scalar equation \(\hat x'^\top E\hat x = 0\), linear in E’s entries (5 is the minimum with the nonlinear constraints of E, 8 with the linear method). From E you recover R and the translation direction; the magnitude of t stays unknown.

3. RANSAC in Algorithm 1. It wraps step 3 (computing E). Model: E (equivalently R and t̂). Data sampled: minimal sets of 5 (or 8) correspondences. Inlier test: point-to-epipolar-line distance (Sampson error) below a threshold. Why not least squares on all matches? Squared error lets a few gross outliers (wrong matches) pull the solution arbitrarily far; least squares assumes small Gaussian noise.

4. Pose ambiguity and scale. E decomposes into 4 (R, t) pairs. Triangulate a point with each; only the physically valid one gives positive depth in both cameras. Even then |t| is unknown, because scaling the scene and t by the same factor gives identical images. Metric scale needs extra information: a stereo baseline, a depth sensor, an IMU, a known object size or camera height, or wheel odometry.

3. 3D–3D: aligning point clouds (Arun et al. 1987)

With 3D points in both frames (stereo or RGB-D) slides p.39, \(x_t^i = R x_{t-1}^i + t\). Minimise \(\sum_i\|x_t^i - (Rx_{t-1}^i + t)\|^2\) slides p.53; with noise the sum is never exactly 0. Decouple rotation from translation slides p.54:

  1. compute the centroids \(\bar x_t, \bar x_{t-1}\) slides p.55;
  2. subtract them from each set slides p.56;
  3. \(H = \sum_i \bar x_{t-1}^i\,\bar x_t^{i\top}\) (cross-covariance, a sum of outer products), \(USV^\top = \mathrm{svd}(H)\), \(R = VU^\top\) (if det R = −1 you have a reflection, a degenerate case) slides p.62;
  4. \(t = \bar x_t - R\,\bar x_{t-1}\) slides p.67.

The camera motion is the inverse of this point-alignment transform slides p.69. Accuracy is limited because stereo depth error is large and anisotropic (a diamond-shaped uncertainty region), so it is better to minimise image-level quantities (reprojection error) as 2D–2D and 3D–2D methods do slides p.70. VO Part I says the same: 3D–2D beats 3D–3D.

The check script confirms this recipe recovers a 0.7 rad rotation and t = (1, 2, 3) exactly on noise-free points.

4. Chaining transforms (frame conventions)

Write \({}^{A}T_{B}\) for the transform that maps coordinates in frame B into frame A. Then \({}^{A}T_{C} = {}^{A}T_{B}\,{}^{B}T_{C}\): the inner indices cancel (practice Q15). Also \({}^{B}T_{A} = ({}^{A}T_{B})^{-1}\). A VO trajectory is a chain: \(C_k = C_{k-1}T_k\).

Worked example (2-D). \({}^{A}T_{B}\) = rotate 90°, then translate (2, 0). \({}^{B}T_{C}\) = translate (1, 0). The origin of C is at (1, 0) in B; rotating by 90° gives (0, 1), and translating gives (2, 1) in A.

5. Keyframes

Frame-to-frame estimates are never exact. A static camera tracked frame to frame drifts like a random walk slides p.75 slides p.76. So estimate motion relative to the last keyframe, \({}^{K_i}T_{C_k}\) slides p.77. Add a new keyframe slides p.78 when the camera has moved or rotated enough, when too many features are lost, or every N frames. Too few keyframes give a poor map; too many waste computation.

Monocular rule of thumb slides p.80: add a keyframe when keyframe distance / average depth > 10–20%. A small baseline relative to scene depth gives large triangulation uncertainty. Pure rotation has no baseline, so nothing can be triangulated.

PTAM case study slides p.87 runs tracking and mapping in separate threads. Map initialisation: 1000 points, 5-point E + RANSAC, triangulate, BA, assume 10 cm of motion for scale, align the dominant plane to z = 0. Tracking: 3D–2D pose updates with a robust Tukey loss (Week 6). Mapping: triangulate new points from keyframes.

Tutorial: full discussion (optional depth)

Minimal solvers and refinement. RANSAC iterations grow exponentially with sample size, so the 3-point P3P keeps iterations low and gives many cheap hypotheses. But a pose fitted exactly to 3 noisy points absorbs all of their noise; refining with all inliers (iterative PnP, LM) averages that noise. In the practical, AP3P inside solvePnPRansac generates and scores hypotheses, and solvePnPRefineLM refines the best one.

Defining an inlier (PnP). Model: the camera pose (R, t). Hypothesis data: 3 (or 4) 3D–2D correspondences. Inlier error: the reprojection distance in pixels between the observed feature and the projected 3D point. A larger threshold keeps more matches, including wrong ones and points with bad depth; a smaller one rejects good but noisy points. For E, the test is a point-to-epipolar-line distance (1-D: it tests consistency with a line); PnP tests the distance to a predicted point (2-D).

Reprojection error. It is measured where the sensor actually measures (pixels), so it matches the noise model. In the 2D–2D practical only s is optimised (R and t̂ are fixed from E); iterative PnP refines all 6 DOF of the pose.

Stereo depth sensitivity. \(\delta Z \approx Z^2\delta d/(fb)\): nearby points are accurate and distant points very uncertain. PnP uses the 3D points directly as fixed “truth”, so depth errors bias the pose. E uses only 2D image coordinates, so stereo depth enters only through the scale.

Outlier rejection. Squared error gives an outlier influence that grows without bound, so a few wrong matches can drag the pose far away. RANSAC removes most of them first. It does not guarantee every retained match is correct: an outlier that happens to lie within the threshold survives, and the inlier set depends on the threshold and on luck.

Drift. A small rotation error rotates every later step; translation-direction and scale errors accumulate along the path. Contributors: feature localisation noise, mismatches that survive, stereo depth error (PnP), the scale estimate (2D–2D) and degenerate geometry (low parallax).

Springs between poses

Picture each camera pose as a bead and each measurement as a spring that wants a certain gap between beads. Let go, and the beads settle where the total spring tension is lowest.

In the practical: each between-pose factor in SymForce is one of these springs.

Confidence = spring stiffness

A precise measurement is a stiff spring (small σ, big weight 1/σ²); a sloppy one is a floppy spring that gives way.

Robust losses

Huber and Tukey are springs that stop pulling harder once they are stretched absurdly far, so one crazy measurement can’t drag everything.

Bundle adjustment

Move both the cameras and the 3D points until every point lands exactly where it was seen in every photo.

MAP

Of all possible trajectories, pick the single most believable one. You lose the “how sure am I” information unless you also keep the spread.

2. Pose-graph optimisation

Relative motion can be estimated between non-consecutive frames too slides p.33. Then \({}^{x_0}T_{x_1}\,{}^{x_1}T_{x_2} \ne {}^{x_0}T_{x_2}\) in general, and the measurements have different confidence slides p.36. Solve for all poses together slides p.39:

\[X^* = \arg\min_X \sum_{i,j}\big\| x_i - {}^{i}T_j\,x_j \big\|^2_{\Sigma_{ij}}\]

The variables are the poses \(\{R_i, t_i\}\); the prediction is \({}^{i}T_j x_j\); the residual is the pose difference (a difference between SE(3) elements, computed on the manifold, not a plain minus); the objective is a weighted sum of squares. Rotations make it nonlinear, so it needs Gauss–Newton or Levenberg–Marquardt.

Worked example (1-D). x0 = 0 is fixed. Odometry says x1 − x0 = 1 and x2 − x1 = 1; a loop closure says x2 − x0 = 1.8; all σ = 0.1. Minimise \((x_1-1)^2 + (x_2-x_1-1)^2 + (x_2-1.8)^2\). Setting the derivatives to zero gives \(2x_1 - x_2 = 0\) and \(-x_1 + 2x_2 = 2.8\), so x1 = 0.933, x2 = 1.867. The 0.2 disagreement is shared equally by the three equally weighted factors (each takes 0.067). Make the loop more precise in the widget and x2 moves towards 1.8.

4. How the solver works

For a simple function, set the derivative to zero: \(f(x)=3x^2+5(x+3)\) gives \(f'(x) = 6x+5 = 0\), so \(x = -5/6\) slides p.48. Real problems have thousands of nonlinear, vector-valued terms, so we iterate slides p.49 slides p.50. Start from a guess and step \(x \leftarrow x + \Delta x\):

  • Gradient descent: step along \(-J^\top r\) (cautious, slow).
  • Gauss–Newton: \((J^\top J)\Delta x = -J^\top r\) (fast near the minimum).
  • Levenberg–Marquardt: \((J^\top J + \lambda I)\Delta x = -J^\top r\), which interpolates between Gauss–Newton and gradient descent slides p.52.

These are all normal equations \(A^\top A x = A^\top b\) slides p.55. The Jacobian is sparse, because each measurement touches only a few variables (the factor-graph structure), so they solve efficiently slides p.54. Spring–mass picture: the solution is the minimum-energy configuration slides p.56.

Keep in mind slides p.57 slides p.58 slides p.59: there can be several minima, and the solution depends on the initial guess, which must be close (practice Q24). Least squares is vulnerable to outliers. Tools: gtsam, Ceres, SymForce (autodiff Jacobians, since hand-written Jacobians are error-prone).

5. Factor graphs, probability and MAP

We estimate hidden variables from measurements and priors and fuse sensors with different noise slides p.63 slides p.64. A Gaussian has mean μ and variance σ² (in N-D, μ and Σ: an ellipse or ellipsoid) slides p.68 slides p.69. Kalman example slides p.71: the prediction after motion is broad, and the measurement pulls the posterior towards whichever input is more certain.

Worked example (fusion). Prior N(5, 1²) and measurement N(6, 0.5²). The weights are 1/σ²: 1 and 4. Posterior mean = (5·1 + 6·4)/5 = 5.8; variance = 1/5 = 0.2 (σ ≈ 0.45), smaller than either input.

Notation slides p.72: p(x) prior, p(x|u) prediction, p(x|u,z) posterior, p(z|x) measurement likelihood = sensor model. Smoothing asks for \(p(x_0..x_t|u,z)\); filtering asks for \(p(x_t|u,z)\) or recursively \(p(x_t|x_{t-1},u_{t-1},z_t)\) slides p.84.

Factor graph slides p.74 slides p.75: variable nodes (unknowns) and factor nodes (probabilistic constraints from measurements or priors). It is bipartite (every edge joins a variable to a factor) and undirected. Examples slides p.77 slides p.79 slides p.81 slides p.82: GPS unary factors, VO binary factors, pose-graph SLAM with a loop closure, SLAM with landmarks, BA. The factors are the terms of the factorised joint distribution, \(\prod_i p(x_i|x_{i-1},u_{i-1})\prod_j p(x_j|z_j)\) slides p.86.

MAP vs the full distribution slides p.89 slides p.90: \(x^* = \arg\max p(x|\ldots)\) is easier to compute, but you lose the uncertainty. The full distribution keeps mean and variance. Two posteriors with the same MAP can have very different spreads, and so warrant different decisions (practice Q25).

The derivation slides p.97–slides p.106: assume Gaussian factors, \(p \propto \exp\!\big(-\tfrac12 e^\top\Sigma^{-1}e\big)\) with \(e = x_i - {}^{i}T_j x_j\). Drop the constant; take the log (monotonic, so the argmax is unchanged, and products become sums); flip the sign and maximise → minimise:

\[X^* = \arg\min_X\sum_{i,j}\|e_{ij}\|^2_{\Sigma_{ij}},\qquad \|e\|^2_\Sigma = e^\top\Sigma^{-1}e\ \ (\text{Mahalanobis distance})\]

MAP estimation of a Gaussian factor graph = weighted nonlinear least squares.

Worked example (practice Q23). Same residual r, σ_A = 0.5, σ_B = 1.0. Contributions \((r/0.5)^2 = 4r^2\) vs \(r^2\), so A contributes 4×. Halving σ quadruples the weight.

Pose graph paper p.9: nodes = camera poses, edges = rigid transforms. Cost \(\sum_{e_{ij}}\|C_i - T_{e_{ij}}C_j\|^2\). The rotation part makes it nonlinear, so the paper suggests Levenberg–Marquardt. Tutorial: full discussion (optional depth)

From pairwise motion to refinement. \(C_k = C_{k-1}T_k\). By first-order error propagation \(\Sigma_k = J_C\Sigma_{k-1}J_C^\top + J_T\Sigma_{k,k-1}J_T^\top\), so uncertainty only grows. Small rotation errors swing every later translation, which is why a 0.5° heading error becomes metres of drift after hundreds of metres.

Non-consecutive frames. Each extra estimate \(T_{k,k-n}\) becomes an extra edge (factor) between non-adjacent nodes. That over-determines the poses, so errors in one edge can be corrected by the others. The frames must share enough visual content (overlapping view, enough common features with good parallax) for a reliable estimate.

Building the graph. Nodes are camera poses; edges are relative-pose measurements with their uncertainty. The optimisation adjusts the poses, and the measurements stay fixed. The error is the difference between each measured relative transform and the one implied by the current poses (Mahalanobis-weighted). It is nonlinear because of rotations; the paper suggests Levenberg–Marquardt.

Uncertainty and outliers. A bad relative transform corrupts every later accumulated pose; weighting each edge by its covariance limits the damage from uncertain ones. Non-consecutive pairs have a wider baseline and more appearance change, so mismatches are more likely, and a wrong edge in a least-squares graph pulls everything. Reject outlier matches (RANSAC) before estimating the edge, and use robust losses or switchable constraints for wrong loop edges.

Pose graph vs BA. PGO: variables = poses; measurements = relative transforms; error = pose residual. BA: variables = poses + landmarks; measurements = 2D observations; error = reprojection error. BA needs tracks over more than two frames, otherwise a landmark is constrained by only one pair and adds nothing beyond two-view geometry. Window size is set by the computation budget.

VO or SLAM? VO keeps the trajectory locally consistent; SLAM keeps it globally consistent, which needs detecting a return to a known place (loop closure), geometric verification, adding a loop constraint and re-optimising the whole graph (often followed by global BA).

3. Evaluating VO: ATE and RPE

Absolute Trajectory Error slides p.40 slides p.41: align the estimate to ground truth with a similarity transform \((R, T, s)\) (least squares over positions), then \(\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\tfrac1n\sum_k\|\hat C_k - sRC_k - T\|^2}\). Pros: one intuitive number in metres that captures accumulated drift and global accuracy. Cons: it needs spatial and temporal alignment; one RMSE hides where and when errors happened; it is sensitive to outliers; it mixes translation drift with rotation-induced error; and it can mislead for monocular VO when the scale is fitted over the whole trajectory. A single early rotation error gives a huge ATE even if everything after is perfect slides p.42.

Relative Pose Error slides p.43 compares frame-to-frame motion: \(E_i = (\hat T_{i-1}^{-1}\hat T_i)^{-1}(T_{i-1}^{-1}T_i)\), \(\text{RPE}_{trans} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{N-1}\sum_i\|\text{trans}(E_i)\|^2}\) (and similarly for rotation). It measures local accuracy and drift rate.

Mean or median? slides p.44 Averages are skewed by outliers, so report the median too, or a boxplot (median, 25th/75th percentiles, whiskers at 5th/95th, dots for outliers).

Worked example. Aligned position errors 0.3, 0.4, 0.0, 1.2 m. RMSE = √((0.09+0.16+0+1.44)/4) = √0.4225 = 0.65 m; mean 0.475 m; median 0.35 m. The single 1.2 m error dominates the RMSE.

4. From VO to visual SLAM

VO is incremental and locally consistent; SLAM keeps a map so that it can recognise revisits and enforce global consistency through loop closure slides p.48. The history slides p.52–slides p.63 runs as follows:

  • Sonar/lidar SLAM built 2-D occupancy grids or landmark maps. Cameras are rich (geometry, texture, semantics), cheap, compact and passive; depth cameras (Kinect) added RGB-D SLAM.

  • KinectFusion (2011): dense RGB-D SLAM with a TSDF map.
  • Event-camera SLAM for HDR and high speed.
  • Semantic SLAM: SemanticFusion (CNN labels fused into a dense map), SLAM++ (objects as landmarks), QuadricSLAM (dual quadrics from detections), 3D scene graphs for large scale.

ORB-SLAM3 (Campos et al., IEEE T-RO 2021)

Problem. One system for visual, visual–inertial and multi-map SLAM with monocular, stereo and RGB-D cameras (pinhole or fisheye). VO uses only the last few seconds; ORB-SLAM3 reuses all previous information, including co-visible keyframes far apart in time or from earlier sessions paper p.2.

Components (Fig. 1). The Atlas holds an active map plus non-active maps and one DBoW2 keyframe database. Tracking computes each frame’s pose against the active map by minimising reprojection error, decides keyframes, and relocalises in all maps when lost. Local mapping adds or culls keyframes and points and runs local (visual–inertial) BA; it also initialises the IMU by MAP estimation. Loop & map merging runs place recognition at keyframe rate: a match in the same map triggers loop correction (pose-graph / essential-graph optimisation, then full BA); a match in another map merges the two.

Headline numbers. 2–10× more accurate than previous VI systems. Stereo-inertial: 3.5 cm average on EuRoC (drone) and 9 mm in TUM-VI room sequences. Raw DBoW2 place recognition has 50–80% precision/recall; with consistency checks it reaches 100% precision at 30–40% recall, which motivated the new, higher-recall method.

How to read it. Accuracy is reported as ATE RMSE after alignment (Fig. 4 colours RMS ATE over 10 runs per EuRoC sequence). Lower is better; the spread across runs shows robustness.

Tutorial: full discussion (optional depth)

1. Main components. Tracking (real-time pose per frame, keyframe decisions, relocalisation), local mapping (insert or cull keyframes and points, local BA, IMU initialisation and refinement), loop closing and map merging (place recognition with DBoW2, loop correction, merging, full BA in a separate thread), and the Atlas (multi-map store with a shared keyframe database).

2. Why keyframes? Consecutive frames are highly redundant. Optimising every frame wastes compute and adds little parallax. Keyframes give well-spread, informative views, bounded map size and real-time BA. Trade-off: too sparse loses tracking and coverage; too dense costs compute. ORB-SLAM inserts generously and culls redundant keyframes later.

3. Where optimisation is used. Tracking optimises the current pose only (motion-only BA). Local BA optimises the keyframes near the current one plus their points, for accuracy at real-time cost. Pose-graph (essential-graph) optimisation spreads a loop correction cheaply over the whole map. Global (full) BA refines all keyframes and points after a loop for the final MAP estimate (in VI mode only if the number of keyframes is below a threshold).

4. Relocalisation vs loop closure vs map merging. Relocalisation: tracking is lost, so find the current frame’s pose in an existing map with no prior pose; this resumes tracking. Loop closure: place recognition matches a keyframe in the same active map; this corrects accumulated drift. Map merging: the match is in a different (non-active) map, e.g. after a new map was started when tracking failed; the maps are fused so information is reused across sessions.

4. Architectures: FC, CNN, ResNet, ViT

A fully connected layer computes \(y = a(W^\top x + b)\) slides p.51; outputs are read as class confidences slides p.55. A convolution applies the same local filter everywhere (weight sharing means fewer parameters and a translation-related inductive bias), and stacking grows the receptive field. CNNs are translation equivariant (shift the input → the feature map shifts), which is not the same as invariant slides p.56. ResNets add skip connections slides p.60.

Vision Transformer slides p.65–slides p.77: cut the image into patches, project each linearly to an embedding, then let tokens update each other with attention. For each token \(q_i = W_qx_i\), \(k_j = W_kx_j\) (“what I contain”, used for matching) and \(v_j = W_vx_j\) (“what I can send”). The score is \(q_i\cdot k_j\); the softmax weights sum to 1; the message is \(z_i = \sum_j a_{ij}v_j\); a residual connection keeps what the token knew: \(x_i' = x_i + z_i\). In matrix form, \(\mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt d)V\). Multi-head attention runs several of these in parallel, and layers stack.

Worked example. Scores 2.4, 2.1, 0.8, −0.5 → softmax weights 0.501, 0.371, 0.101, 0.028. A 224×224 image with P = 16 gives 196 tokens; P = 8 gives 784 tokens and (784/196)² = 16× the attention pairs.

Tutorial paper · in 60 seconds

An Image is Worth 16×16 Words: ViT (Dosovitskiy et al., ICLR 2021)

Problem. Can a standard Transformer work directly on images with minimal changes? Yes: treat image patches as tokens.

Key idea (Sec. 3.1, Fig. 1 paper p.3): reshape \(x\in\mathbb R^{H\times W\times C}\) into \(N = HW/P^2\) flattened patches; project each linearly to D dims (patch embeddings); prepend a learnable [class] token whose final state, after LayerNorm, is the image representation; add learnable 1-D position embeddings (2-D-aware ones gave no significant gain). The head is an MLP during pre-training and a single linear layer for fine-tuning.

Inductive bias. CNNs build in locality, 2-D neighbourhood structure and translation equivariance in every layer. In ViT only the MLPs are local; self-attention is global, and 2-D structure is used only when cutting patches and when interpolating position embeddings for a new resolution. So ViT must learn spatial relations from data.

Headline numbers. Trained on ImageNet alone (mid-sized) it is a few % below comparable ResNets. Pre-trained on ImageNet-21k or JFT-300M, “large scale training trumps inductive bias”: ViT-H/14 reaches 88.55% ImageNet top-1. Variants: Base (12 layers, D = 768, 86M params), Large (24, 1024, 307M), Huge (32, 1280, 632M). Fig. 3: pre-trained on ImageNet, ViT-L does worse than ViT-B; only JFT-300M shows the full benefit of large models.

Tutorial: full discussion (optional depth)

1. Image → sequence. Split the image into P×P patches (N = HW/P²), flatten each (P²·C numbers), project linearly to a D-dim token, prepend the class token and add position embeddings so the model knows where each patch came from, since attention itself is order-agnostic.

2. Through the Transformer. Each layer: LayerNorm → multi-head self-attention → residual → LayerNorm → MLP → residual. Attention computes, for each token, a weighted mix of all tokens’ values, with weights from query·key similarity. So a wheel patch can absorb information from a distant forklift-body patch in one layer.

3. Class token. A learnable extra token with no image content. Through attention it gathers a summary of all patches; its final output feeds the classification head.

4. Missing biases. Locality, 2-D neighbourhoods and translation equivariance are hard-wired in CNNs but must be learned by ViT, which therefore needs more data (or strong regularisation or pre-training) to generalise.

5. Patch size. Small patches keep fine detail but give long sequences, and attention cost grows with N² (halve P → 4× tokens → 16× attention). Large patches are cheap but blur small structures (thin poles, distant obstacles).

6. Why pre-training helps. Large datasets let the model learn the regularities that CNN biases would supply for free, and then transfer them. It underfits small data less well than a CNN would.

7. Global attention in robotics. It is useful for context and long-range relations (is this wheel part of a forklift? does the corridor continue?). Local structure still matters for precise boundaries, small objects and geometry (feature localisation, depth edges).

8. Reuse. The patch-token backbone transfers to detection (DETR-style decoders with queries), segmentation (a per-token decoder upsampled to pixels), depth (dense prediction heads such as DPT) and place recognition (aggregating patch features as in AnyLoc). Each needs a task head, sometimes a decoder for dense outputs, and the matching loss and metric.

1. Monocular depth prediction

A depth map gives, for each pixel, the Z coordinate of its 3D point in the camera frame (H×W×1) slides p.5. Training signals slides p.6:

  • Direct supervision: GT depth from LiDAR, RGB-D, structured light, stereo or synthetic rendering. It can learn metric depth, but dense accurate GT is expensive at scale slides p.8.
  • Self-supervision from geometry: stereo pairs or video with known motion. Predict depth, warp one view into the other and minimise photometric error.
  • Pseudo-labels: a strong teacher labels a large unlabelled set and a student learns from it.

Monocular depth is ill-posed: infinitely many scenes give the same image, and the global scale stays ambiguous (room vs doll’s house). Hence scale-invariant losses and the trend towards relative depth slides p.10.

Stereo self-supervision slides p.12 slides p.13: the network predicts disparity from the left image. Pixel (u, v) with disparity d corresponds to (u − d, v) in the right image. The photometric loss compares \(I_{left}(u,v)\) with \(I_{right}(u-d, v)\), so no depth labels are needed.

Worked example. Predicted disparity 12 px at (200, 80) → compare with the right image at (188, 80). If the network had predicted 10 px, it would sample (190, 80), probably a different intensity, and the loss pushes the disparity towards the value that makes the pixels agree.

Tutorial paper · in 60 seconds

DUSt3R: Geometric 3D Vision Made Easy (Wang et al., CVPR 2024)

Problem. Classical multi-view reconstruction chains calibration → matching → pose → triangulation, and each stage can fail. DUSt3R regresses 3D directly from an image pair with no known intrinsics or poses.

Key idea (Sec. 3.1, Fig. 2 paper p.3): a Siamese, weight-sharing ViT encoder, two transformer decoders that exchange information through cross-attention, and regression heads. The output is two pointmaps \(X^{1,1}, X^{2,1}\) both expressed in camera 1’s frame, plus confidence maps. The loss is a 3-D regression normalised by the mean point distance (to handle scale), weighted by confidence C with a −α log C regulariser. Low confidence is learned without supervision, e.g. for sky and translucent surfaces.

What falls out (Sec. 3.3): matches = mutual nearest neighbours in 3-D; focal length by fitting \(f\) so that \(f\cdot(X,Y)/Z\) matches pixel offsets (principal point assumed at the centre); relative pose by Procrustes (closed form, noise-sensitive) or more robustly PnP + RANSAC. Global alignment (Sec. 3.4): optimise world pointmaps, a pose \(P_e\) and scale \(\sigma_e\) per pair (with \(\prod\sigma_e = 1\) to avoid the trivial σ = 0), minimising 3-D errors by gradient descent. It is fast, unlike 2-D reprojection BA.

Headline numbers (Sec. 4.5, DTU, zero-shot, no camera parameters): accuracy 2.7 mm, completeness 0.8 mm, overall 1.7 mm. That is below the best MVS methods, which use GT poses, train on DTU and triangulate with sub-pixel precision; regression is less accurate. The authors argue the plug-and-play convenience is worth it.

Tutorial: full discussion (optional depth)

1. Pointmap vs depth map. A depth map stores Z per pixel; turning it into 3D needs the intrinsics K (and the pose, for a world frame). A pointmap stores (X, Y, Z) per pixel directly in a chosen frame.

2. Common frame. Expressing both images’ points in camera 1’s frame makes them directly comparable: matching becomes a 3-D nearest-neighbour search and relative pose a point-set alignment. The network must implicitly learn the relative pose and the correspondence between the views (the epipolar relationship).

3. What is replaced or relaxed. Feature matching, calibration, pose estimation and triangulation are all replaced by one regression. What remains: the pinhole projection (used to recover focal length and to build GT), multi-view consistency, scale ambiguity (pointmaps are normalised) and global alignment.

4. Confidence maps. They weight the loss and signal reliability. Expect low confidence in sky, reflective or transparent surfaces, textureless regions, object boundaries and areas seen in only one view.

5. Global alignment. Build a pair graph, predict pointmaps per pair and optimise world pointmaps with one rigid transform and one scale per pair so that all pairs agree (confidence-weighted 3-D error). Scale factors are needed because each pair is predicted up to its own normalised scale.

6. Why recover intrinsics, poses and depth? Downstream robotics still needs them: planning in metric space, fusing with other sensors, using the camera in a SLAM or BA pipeline, checking consistency, or re-using classical refinement.

7. Recovering quantities (Sec. 3.3). Matching: reciprocal 3-D nearest neighbours. Focal: robust least-squares fit of f to the pointmap’s x/z, y/z against pixel offsets. Relative pose: Procrustes on the two pointmaps of the same image, or PnP-RANSAC. One representation serves all these tasks, hence “a common representation”.

8. MVS results (Sec. 4.5). 2.7 mm accuracy, 0.8 mm completeness, 1.7 mm overall on DTU, behind methods with GT cameras. The authors explain this by those methods using known poses, DTU training and sub-pixel triangulation, while regression is less precise. They trade some accuracy for needing no prior camera information.

1. The detection task

Input an image; output a set of boxes \((x_{min}, y_{min}, x_{max}, y_{max})\), each with a class-score distribution slides p.9 slides p.10. The key challenge slides p.13: the number of outputs depends on the image, unlike classification or segmentation. Candidate objects come from slides p.14:

  1. two-stage detectors: anchors + a region proposal network;
  2. anchor-based one-stage: predefined reference boxes;
  3. anchor-free / point-based: every image location predicts;
  4. transformers: learned object queries.

2. Two-stage detectors (Faster R-CNN)

A reliable, modular baseline that is easy to fine-tune (Detectron2, MMDetection, TorchVision). Descendants: Mask R-CNN, Cascade R-CNN, Sparse R-CNN slides p.17.

Stage 1: a dense grid of anchors at each location (3 scales × 3 aspect ratios). The RPN predicts an objectness score and a box refinement for each; keep the top-N (e.g. 1,000) proposals slides p.18–slides p.21. Stage 2: RoI pooling turns regions of different sizes into fixed-size features slides p.24; the head predicts class probabilities (including background) and refines the box slides p.26.

Post-processing slides p.32: drop low-confidence predictions, then remove duplicates with non-maximum suppression (keep the highest-scoring box and suppress same-class boxes with IoU above a threshold). DETR-style detectors generally need no NMS.

3. One-stage and point-based detectors

These go straight from image to detections slides p.35. Anchor-based: SSD, RetinaNet, EfficientDet (less dominant now). Point-based: every pixel predicts class scores and box geometry: FCOS, CornerNet, CenterNet; currently YOLOv8, YOLO11, RTMDet.

FCOS slides p.37–slides p.42 is anchor-free and proposal-free, so it has no anchor hyperparameters (which are sensitive) and no anchor-overlap computation. Per pixel it predicts class scores and a centerness score; their product gives centerness-aware scores. It regresses the box from each pixel, then thresholds away low-confidence pixels.

5. Training detectors

Detection = classification + localisation + assignment (which prediction is responsible for which ground-truth object) slides p.49. Losses:

  • Classification slides p.50: CE (softmax), BCE (sigmoid), or focal loss for heavy imbalance.
  • Box regression slides p.51: L1 on coordinates, or IoU-based losses (1 − IoU, GIoU, DIoU, CIoU). Coordinate losses ask whether the numbers are close; IoU losses ask whether the boxes overlap well.
  • Objectness slides p.52: is there any object here (RPN, YOLO)?

Class imbalance slides p.53: thousands of candidates, mostly background, and easy negatives dominate the loss. Remedies: sampling, focal loss (down-weights easy, correct examples), DETR’s one-to-one matching. Datasets slides p.54: COCO (80 classes), Objects365 (365 classes, 2M images, 30M boxes), OpenImages V7 (600 classes, 15.9M boxes), LVIS (1200+ classes, long tail), nuImages (driving).

6. Evaluating detectors: IoU, precision, recall, AP

\(\mathrm{IoU} = \frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|} = \frac{I}{|A|+|B|-I}\). A detection is a true positive if IoU ≥ threshold, the class is correct and that ground-truth object was not already matched by a higher-scoring detection; every other detection is a false positive; every unmatched ground-truth object is a false negative slides p.59–slides p.62.

Worked example (practice Q31). Box areas 60 and 50, intersection 30: IoU = 30/(60+50−30) = 30/80 = 0.375. Boxes (0,0,4,4) and (2,2,6,6): I = 4, U = 16+16−4 = 28 → 0.143.

\(\text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP}\) (what fraction of detections are right) and \(\text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN}\) (what fraction of objects were found). Both depend on the confidence threshold and the IoU threshold slides p.66. Sweeping the confidence threshold traces a PR curve. AP = area under the PR curve for one class at one IoU threshold (COCO samples it at 101 recall values). mAP = mean AP over classes, \(\frac1k\sum AP_i\) slides p.67. COCO “mAP” also averages over IoU thresholds 0.5:0.95 slides p.68.

Worked example (AP). 5 ground-truth objects; detections sorted by confidence are TP, TP, FP, TP, FP, FP, TP. Precision: 1, 1, 0.667, 0.75, 0.6, 0.5, 0.571. Recall: 0.2, 0.4, 0.4, 0.6, 0.6, 0.6, 0.8. Making precision monotone (max to the right) and summing over recall steps: 0.2·1 + 0.2·1 + 0.2·0.75 + 0.2·0.571 = AP = 0.664. One object was never found, so recall stops at 0.8.

Caveats for robotics slides p.69 slides p.70: AP summarises many thresholds, while a robot runs at one (practice Q32). mAP punishes low recall strongly. The IoU threshold may be stricter than the task needs. mAP weights every class equally, whatever its real importance. Also check recall at a fixed false-positive rate, latency, distance-dependent recall and safety-critical classes. Choose the threshold for the application slides p.91: high recall for safety, high precision if false alarms trigger costly actions.

7. Open set and open vocabulary

Closed-set training uses a fixed class list (COCO: 80) slides p.73. In deployment, novel objects appear and are often labelled as a known class slides p.75.

  • Closed set: choose among known classes.
  • Open set: detect known classes and flag novel objects as unknown.
  • Open vocabulary: detect objects described by text, beyond the trained list slides p.77.

How: CLIP aligns image and text embeddings, so similarity answers “does this image match this concept?”. Detection also needs “where?”, so propose regions or queries and score each against text embeddings; the head becomes language-conditioned slides p.80–slides p.83. Grounding DINO builds on DETR/DINO, aligns queries to phrases, and lets you change the vocabulary at inference slides p.84. Prompts matter slides p.85: wording changes which objects are found, their labels and confidences, and more specific prompts aren’t always better. Limits slides p.86: visual difficulty (small, occluded, domain shift), language difficulty (synonyms, fine-grained classes), attributes and state (“the empty cup”), relations and affordances (“something I can grasp”). Open vocabulary ≠ open-ended reasoning. Others: OWL-ViT/OWLv2, GLIP, YOLO-World, YOLOE slides p.87.

Practical advice slides p.89: start simple with a strong pretrained baseline (Faster R-CNN) and fine-tune; evaluate the errors that matter.

Tutorial paper · in 60 seconds

End-to-End Object Detection with Transformers: DETR (Carion et al., ECCV 2020)

Problem. Detectors depend on hand-designed parts: anchors, proposal heuristics and NMS. DETR treats detection as direct set prediction.

Key idea (Fig. 2, Sec. 3 paper p.6): a CNN backbone (ResNet-50), a transformer encoder and a decoder with N = 100 learned object queries. A shared FFN turns each query into a class (including ∅ “no object”) and a box (centre, height, width). Training uses Hungarian matching: find the permutation that minimises \(\sum_i \mathcal L_{match}\), where \(\mathcal L_{match} = -\mathbb 1_{c_i\neq\varnothing}\hat p(c_i) + \mathbb 1_{c_i\neq\varnothing}\mathcal L_{box}\) and \(\mathcal L_{box} = \lambda_{iou}\,\text{GIoU} + \lambda_{L1}\|b-\hat b\|_1\). The Hungarian loss is then applied to the matched pairs; unmatched queries learn to predict ∅ (down-weighted ×10 for imbalance). One-to-one assignment penalises duplicates, so no NMS is needed.

Headline numbers (Table 1, COCO val): DETR (41M parameters, 86 GFLOPs, 28 FPS) gets AP 42.0, matching a tuned Faster R-CNN-FPN+ (42.0). APL is 61.1 vs 53.4 (+7.8, global attention helps large objects), but APS is 20.5 vs 26.6 (−5.5, small objects suffer). Loss ablation (Table 4): class + L1 only gives 35.8 AP; class + GIoU 39.9; both 40.6.

Limitations: long training (300–500 epochs), weak on small objects, and a maximum of N detections.

Tutorial: full discussion (optional depth)

1. What DETR removes. Anchor generation, proposal heuristics and NMS post-processing. Treating detection as predicting a set (order-free, fixed size N, padded with ∅) and training it with a one-to-one bipartite matching makes a single forward pass give final detections.

2. Object queries. N = 100 learned embeddings fed to the decoder. Each attends to the image features (and to the other queries) and outputs one prediction: a class distribution including ∅, and a box. Queries not matched to any ground truth are trained to predict “no object”.

3. Matching during training. The Hungarian algorithm finds the lowest-cost one-to-one assignment between predictions and ground truth (padded with ∅). The cost uses the predicted class probability and box similarity (L1 + GIoU). One-to-one assignment means each object supervises exactly one query.

4. Why no NMS. If two queries predicted the same object, only one would be matched; the other would be pushed towards ∅. Through self-attention between queries the decoder learns to avoid duplicates, so there is nothing for NMS to remove.

5. Limitations and evidence. Very long training (300 epochs baseline, 500 for the main comparison, vs a 9× schedule for Faster R-CNN). Small objects: APS 20.5 vs 26.6 for Faster R-CNN-FPN+ (Table 1), linked to single-scale, low-resolution features and global attention cost. Fixed N caps the detections per image (they visualise the 100 slots). The later Deformable DETR and DINO address these.

6. Versus conventional detectors. Conventional detectors represent candidates as anchors or proposals at many positions, regress offsets, and handle duplicates with NMS. DETR’s queries are global, learned slots with no fixed spatial prior; boxes are predicted directly in normalised coordinates; duplicates are handled by the set loss rather than by post-processing.

Tutorial paper · in 60 seconds

Contact-GraspNet (Sundermeyer, Mousavian, Triebel, Fox, ICRA 2021)

Problem. 6-DoF grasping of unknown objects in clutter. Previous pipelines first segment the object, generate grasps on the isolated object cloud, then filter collisions with another network. That causes (1) sensitivity to segmentation errors, (2) no scene context (table, neighbours) for collisions and (3) prediction in a huge, unconstrained 6-DoF space paper p.2.

Key idea: treat each observed 3D point as a potential contact point c. A grasp is \(t_g = c + \tfrac{w}{2}b + d\,a\), \(R_g = [\,b,\ a\times b,\ a\,]\) (a = approach vector, b = baseline vector, d = fixed gripper offset). Per point the network only predicts contact confidence, a, b and the width w: 4-DoF instead of 6-DoF + width, which makes learning much easier and ties grasps to the observed geometry. It runs on the full scene point cloud (PointNet++), so collisions are considered during generation; segmentation is optional, only to choose the target.

Headline numbers. Trained on 17.7M simulated grasps (ACRONYM, 8,872 ShapeNet meshes), class-agnostic. Real Franka robot, 9 cluttered scenes, 51 unseen objects (Table I paper p.6): success 90.20% vs 80.39% for the segmentation + CollisionNet pipeline (62.7% for 6-DOF GraspNet), 84.31% on the first attempt, 59 vs 67 attempts. That is “over 90%”, halving the failure rate. Runtime 0.28 s per scene.

How to read it. Sim results report success (no collision, object held after shaking) against coverage (% of ground-truth grasps within 2 cm of a prediction). Ablations: cropping local regions raises coverage; training on a small 5-category dataset fails to generalise; the weighted width-bin loss prevents collapse to narrow widths.

Tutorial: full discussion (optional depth)

1. Motivation. Multi-stage pipelines (segment → per-object grasps → collision check) have many failure points: segmentation errors (over/under-segmentation) wreck the grasps; grasps generated on an isolated object can’t see the table or neighbours, so many collide; the 6-DoF search space is large and slow, unsuitable for closed-loop grasping.

2. Representation. Each grasp is anchored at an observed contact point c, so its position is mostly known. The network predicts the approach and baseline directions (3 DoF of rotation) and the width w. The full pose follows from \(t_g = c + \tfrac w2 b + d a\) and \(R_g = [b, a\times b, a]\). Sampling contacts across the visible surface covers the modes of the grasp distribution.

3. Whole-scene input. The full cloud includes the table and neighbouring objects, so the network learns which grasps would collide and avoids them during generation. Grasps are found even if segmentation is wrong (the under-segmented drill, Fig. 7); masks are needed only to pick which object’s grasps to execute.

4. Training and sim-to-real. 17.7M physics-simulated parallel-jaw grasps on 8,872 ShapeNet meshes (ACRONYM), placed in random stable cluttered scenes and rendered as depth, class-agnostic. Noise injection helps real sensors. Transfer is shown on a real Franka Panda with a RealSense L515 and 51 unseen objects: 90.2% success.

5. Evidence. Ablations: the weighted width loss is crucial (without it, widths collapse narrow); local regions increase coverage; a large, diverse dataset is essential (the small 5-category dataset fails). Comparison: 90.2% vs 80.4% (segmentation + CollisionNet) and 62.7% (6-DOF GraspNet), with more first-try successes. Remaining limits: thick objects needing near-maximum width get low confidence; small objects have low-confidence contacts; parallel-jaw only; no task semantics.

VPR = photo search

You show the robot a photo and it searches its album for the most similar picture of the same place, even if it’s now night, winter or seen from another angle.

Global vs local descriptors

Global: one summary sentence per photo (fast to compare, misses details). Local: a list of distinctive details (robust to viewpoint, slower to compare).

Bag of visual words

Turn each local feature into a “word” from a dictionary and compare photos by word counts, down-weighting words that appear everywhere (like “the”).

Contrastive learning

Teach the network that two photos of the same corner belong together and a lookalike corner somewhere else does not, especially the tricky lookalikes (hard negatives).

1. Why visual place recognition

VPR asks whether the robot has seen this place before slides p.5. Uses slides p.6: loop-closure detection (SLAM), the kidnapped robot problem, and initial candidates for full 6-DoF localisation (PnP). Why not GNSS slides p.7? It is denied indoors and underground; non-line-of-sight and multipath signals in urban canyons and under canopy degrade it; and it may not be accurate enough.

VPR is an image retrieval problem slides p.9 slides p.10: given a query, find the most similar database images, where “similar” should mean the same physical place despite appearance or viewpoint change. Why not classification? slides p.14 slides p.15 It needs a fixed set of place classes and retraining for new places, and it gives a label rather than a ranking. Retrieval simply inserts new reference images, returns ranked candidates, scales to large changing sets, and handles graded similarity between nearby places.

With ground-truth matches, VPR is a binary decision slides p.46: \(P = \frac{TP}{TP+FP}\) (100 matches, 65 correct → P = 0.65) slides p.48; \(R = \frac{TP}{GTP}\), where GTP counts queries that have a true match (100 found of 200 → R = 0.5) slides p.50. TF-IDF slides p.63: \(t_i = \frac{n_{id}}{n_d}\log\frac{N}{n_i}\). Words that are common everywhere get low weight.

Worked example (slide numbers). N = 1000 images, 100 words per image. w1 appears 10× in this image and 100× overall: \(0.1\cdot\log_{10}(1000/100) = 0.1\). w2 appears 10× here but 10,000× overall: \(0.1\cdot\log_{10}(0.1) = -0.1\). So w2 is a common word and carries much less (here even negative) weight. With the standard definition \(n_i \le N\), the weight would be ≥ 0, but the conclusion is the same: w1 is more informative.

5. Learning descriptors

Same place should be close and different places far apart slides p.69–slides p.72. Options: use a pretrained representation (ImageNet CNNs, DINO) without VPR training; metric / contrastive learning (contrastive, triplet, InfoNCE); or place-classification training (CosPlace). Triplet loss slides p.73 slides p.74: anchor, positive (same place), negative (different place); pull positives together and push negatives apart, e.g. \(\max(0, d(a,p) - d(a,n) + m)\).

Challenges slides p.75: choose negatives that are neither too easy nor too hard; decide how far you must move before it counts as a new place; GPS distance usually defines positives and negatives, but viewpoint means geographically close images can look very different; hard-negative mining matters because lookalike places are the informative examples.

VPR is only the first step slides p.77 slides p.78 slides p.80: retrieval generates candidates. Tutorial paper · in 60 seconds

AnyLoc: Towards Universal Visual Place Recognition (Keetha et al., 2023)

Problem. The best VPR methods are trained for one environment, mostly urban driving, and degrade badly in unstructured settings. AnyLoc aims to be universal: anywhere (urban, indoor, aerial, underwater, subterranean, degraded), anytime (day–night, seasons) and anyview (including > 90° viewpoint shifts), without any VPR-specific training or fine-tuning paper p.1.

Key idea. Use dense per-pixel features from an off-the-shelf self-supervised foundation model (DINOv2 ViT-G, layer 31, value facet, which gives the sharpest contrast) and aggregate them without supervision: \(F_G = (\sum f_i^{\,p})^{1/p}\) (p = 1 GAP, p = 3 GeM, p → ∞ GMP), or VLAD (k-means vocabulary over database features, summing residuals \(f_i - c_k\) per cluster, hard or soft assignment) paper p.3.

Domains. A PCA of DINOv2-GeM descriptors separates datasets into domains (Urban, Indoor, Aerial, SubT, Degraded, Underwater). Building domain-specific VLAD vocabularies gives a further ~6%. Features from VPR-trained MixVPR collapse into a small region of feature space.

Headline numbers. Evaluated on 12 diverse datasets with Recall@1 (and R@5): up to 4× higher Recall@1 than supervised state-of-the-art baselines (NetVLAD, CosPlace, MixVPR), especially in unstructured environments. Takeaway: self-supervised features and unsupervised aggregation are both crucial, and aggregated per-pixel features beat per-image (CLS) features.

Tutorial: full discussion (optional depth)

1. Motivation and “universal”. Today’s strong VPR models are environment- and task-specific (trained on urban street views) and brittle elsewhere. Universal means one method that works across very different places, times and viewpoints with no retraining or fine-tuning for each new domain.

2. Features. Dense patch features from large self-supervised models (DINO, DINOv2; CLIP was also considered). They were trained on huge, diverse data without labels, so they encode general visual semantics and structure instead of one dataset’s biases, and they stay discriminative across domains. The chosen layer/facet (ViT-G layer 31, value) gives the sharpest similarity contrast.

3. Local → global. Aggregate the per-pixel features into one vector: GAP, GMP, GeM (generalised mean) and VLAD (hard and soft assignment to a k-means vocabulary, residual sums, then normalisation). VLAD on DINOv2 features performs best.

4. Evaluating generalisation. 12 datasets covering structured (urban, indoor) and unstructured (aerial, underwater, subterranean, degraded) environments, day/night and seasonal change, and opposing viewpoints, measured by Recall@1 and R@5 against supervised baselines (NetVLAD, CosPlace, MixVPR). Evidence: large gains (up to 4× R@1) on unstructured sets, competitive on urban ones.

5. Domains. A PCA of global descriptors reveals clusters that match environment types; queries land near their database. AnyLoc builds a separate VLAD vocabulary per domain, which improves results by about 6%.

Optional discussion: is “universal” justified? The evidence is broad (12 datasets), but untested conditions include thermal or event cameras, heavy rain, snow and fog, forests and agricultural fields with self-similar vegetation, crowded dynamic scenes, long-term structural change (construction), and very large city-scale databases where ViT-G cost and VLAD descriptor size matter. A cautious reading is “broadly general” rather than proven universal.

1. The problem: closed-set training, open-world deployment

Mistakes range from annoying to life-threatening slides p.4. Models are trained under closed-set conditions and deployed under open-set / OOD conditions slides p.5. The typical failure is that unknown objects are confidently misclassified as a known class slides p.8.

Definitions slides p.10: OOD means the input comes from a different distribution (where \(p_{train}(x)\) is tiny or zero); open-set recognition means unseen classes appear at test time and should be recognised as unknown; open-world recognition also learns those new classes over time. An OOD input need not be a new class: a known class in heavy fog, or seen by a new sensor, is OOD too.

Assumption: \(p_{train}(x,y) = p_{deploy}(x,y)\) slides p.11. The types of shift slides p.12:

ShiftWhat changesExample
covariate\(p(x)\) changes, \(p(y|x)\) samelighting, weather, camera, corruption
label / prior\(p(y)\) changes, \(p(x|y)\) samesome classes become much more common
concept\(p(y|x)\) changesthe meaning of an appearance changes
novel classes (open set)\(p_{train}(x,y^*)=0\), \(p_{deploy}>0\)a new object type

Confidence feeds downstream decisions (fusion, trust, fallback, weighting in estimation), so miscalibration makes those decisions systematically wrong slides p.13. Three different questions slides p.14: failure / risk estimation (“should the robot trust this prediction?”, decision-centric), OOD detection (“how unfamiliar is the input?”, data-centric), uncertainty estimation (“how unsure is the model?”, model-centric).

2. Confidence and calibration

Softmax outputs look like probabilities but carry no guarantee slides p.17. Calibrated: n% of predictions made with n% confidence are correct slides p.18. Reliability diagram slides p.19: store correctness and confidence, bin confidences (e.g. 10 bins), plot mean accuracy per bin. Modern networks are often accurate but poorly calibrated, usually over-confident slides p.20.

Worked example (practice Q38). A bin of 10 predictions with mean confidence 0.60; 8 correct → accuracy 0.80 > 0.60 → under-confident by 0.20. A bin at confidence 0.90 with 70/100 correct → over-confident by 0.20.

Expected calibration error (not on the slides, but standard): ECE = Σ (n_b/N)·|acc_b − conf_b|. Bins (conf 0.95, acc 0.80, n 40), (0.75, 0.70, 30), (0.55, 0.60, 30) give ECE = 0.4·0.15 + 0.3·0.05 + 0.3·0.05 = 0.09.

Temperature scaling slides p.21: \(p_i = \frac{e^{z_i/T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j/T}}\), with one scalar T learned on held-out validation data. T > 1 softens over-confident predictions; T < 1 sharpens. All logits are scaled equally, so the predicted class never changes. It is cheap, but calibration fitted in-distribution may not hold under shift.

Worked example. Logits (2, 1, 0): T = 1 → (0.665, 0.245, 0.090); T = 2 → (0.506, 0.307, 0.186). The argmax is still class 1, and the entropy rises from 0.83 to 1.02 nats.

Confidence is the simplest signal slides p.22: maximum softmax probability or detector score, rejecting low maximum confidence or high entropy. It is surprisingly good, but networks can still be confidently wrong under shift, and even calibrated confidence is not a reliable OOD detector.

3. Uncertainty: aleatoric and epistemic

EpistemicAleatoric
lack of knowledge (model uncertainty)noise inherent in the observations

Definitions from slides p.30 (Kendall & Gal).

Learning aleatoric uncertainty slides p.31: the network predicts \(\hat x_i\) and \(\sigma_i^2\) with loss \(\mathcal L = \frac1D\sum_i\frac{\|x_i-\hat x_i\|^2}{2\sigma_i^2} + \frac12\log\sigma_i^2\). A large σ down-weights the error, but the log term penalises claiming large σ everywhere.

Estimating uncertainty slides p.32–slides p.35: predictive entropy \(H(p) = -\sum_c p_c\log p_c\) (high = spread out); deep ensembles (train several models from different initialisations, average; their disagreement captures epistemic uncertainty); MC dropout (keep dropout on at test time, run many passes; the spread measures uncertainty). Uncertainty can be high even for an ambiguous in-distribution image.

4. Out-of-distribution detection

OOD detection is input-centric: is this input atypical relative to the training data slides p.38? Ideas slides p.39 slides p.40: the max-softmax baseline (low max → maybe unfamiliar, but it can be confidently wrong); feature-space distance to training examples or class prototypes; the energy score \(E(x) = -\log\sum_c \exp z_c(x)\) on raw logits. Softmax discards the absolute logit scale, so two inputs can have the same softmax confidence but very different energy; low energy means strong evidence for known classes.

Worked example. Logits (2, 1, 0) and (6, 5, 4) give the same softmax, but energies of −2.41 and −6.41. The second input has much stronger evidence and is more in-distribution.

OOD ≠ failure slides p.41: an OOD input can still be classified correctly, an in-distribution input can be wrong, and high uncertainty, OOD-ness and failure are related but not the same.

5. Decision making

Perception gives a prediction plus confidence, uncertainty and OOD signals → an estimated risk → an action slides p.43. When reliability is low: abstain, fall back to safe behaviour, ask a human, stop or defer; if it persists, collect data and retrain. When it is uncertain: slow down, take another observation, change viewpoint, use temporal evidence, consult another sensor or model. When it is high: act normally slides p.44. Uncertainty is only useful if it changes behaviour.

Selective prediction slides p.45 slides p.46: if \(r(x) < \tau\), predict; else abstain. Coverage = the fraction of inputs predicted on; (selective) risk = the error rate on accepted predictions. Lowering coverage can lower risk (the risk–coverage curve).

Worked example (practice Q39). 50 inputs, 10 abstained, 40 predictions, 4 wrong → coverage 40/50 = 0.80, selective risk 4/40 = 0.10. (Not 4/50 = 0.08: risk is measured on accepted predictions only.)

There is no universal threshold slides p.47: it depends on the consequence and reversibility of a failure, whether more information is available, time constraints and the ability to recover. Warehouse classification: accept moderate uncertainty. Grasping: take another view or replan. Obstacle detection: favour high recall. Autonomous driving: slow down, stop or hand over.

What Uncertainties Do We Need in Bayesian Deep Learning for Computer Vision? (Kendall & Gal, NeurIPS 2017)

Problem. Vision networks give no sense of when they’re wrong. Which uncertainty should we model? Epistemic (model; explained away by data) or aleatoric (observation noise; homoscedastic = constant, heteroscedastic = input-dependent) paper p.2.

Key ideas. MC dropout for epistemic uncertainty: keep dropout at test time, sample T stochastic passes (they used 50), and the spread is the uncertainty (approximate variational inference). Heteroscedastic aleatoric: the network outputs σ² and uses \(\frac1D\sum\tfrac12 e^{-s_i}\|y_i-\hat y_i\|^2 + \tfrac12 s_i\) with \(s = \log\sigma^2\), i.e. learned loss attenuation (like a robust loss). Noisy inputs get less weight, and the \(s\) term stops it predicting high uncertainty everywhere paper p.5. Combining both gives state-of-the-art segmentation (CamVid, NYUv2) and depth (Make3D, NYUv2).

Tutorial: full discussion (optional depth)

1. Aleatoric vs epistemic, with examples. Aleatoric: sensor noise and inherent ambiguity, e.g. depth at object boundaries, a motion-blurred image, distant small pedestrians, reflective floors. Epistemic: lack of knowledge, e.g. a warehouse robot trained indoors driving outside at night, or a new type of forklift never seen in training.

2. Behaviour with more data. Epistemic uncertainty falls as data covers the input space (the model becomes sure which function fits). Aleatoric uncertainty stays, because noise in the observation can’t be removed by more examples of the same noise. Table 3 confirms this: epistemic 1.96 → 1.36 as data grows ×4; aleatoric roughly constant.

3. Estimating epistemic uncertainty. Bayesian NN approximated by MC dropout: dropout before every weight layer during training and at test time; T stochastic forward passes sample weights from the approximate posterior; the variance (or entropy) of the outputs is the epistemic uncertainty. At test time, dropout turns one network into an ensemble of thinned networks.

4. Heteroscedastic regression. The network predicts a mean and a per-input variance and is trained with the Gaussian negative log-likelihood. The residual is divided by σ², so noisy inputs matter less (learned attenuation). The \(\tfrac12\log\sigma^2\) term grows with σ, so predicting huge uncertainty everywhere is penalised. Predicting tiny σ on a large residual is penalised too, through the first term.

5. Unfamiliar inputs. Section 5.2 / Table 3: models trained on CamVid and tested on NYUv2 show much higher epistemic uncertainty, so epistemic is what flags situations never seen. The authors state aleatoric does not increase for out-of-data examples (although the table shows a moderate rise).

6. When each matters. Aleatoric: large-data regimes (epistemic explained away) and real-time systems (no sampling cost). Epistemic: safety-critical systems (detecting novel situations) and small datasets.

Things to think about. Does high uncertainty mean OOD? No: an ambiguous in-distribution image (blur, boundary) gives high aleatoric uncertainty. Does OOD mean a wrong prediction? No: a known class in fog may still be classified correctly. What should the robot do? Act on risk: slow down, take another view or sensor reading, abstain or hand over, log the data for retraining, with the action tied to the cost of failure.

W1 Images

  • img[v,u] = [row, col]; origin top-left; uint8 0–255.
  • Chromaticity r = R/(R+G+B) (white ≈ 0.33).

W2 Features

  • Good feature: distinctive, repeatable, well localised.
  • Harris A = w∗[Jᵤ² JᵤJᵥ; JᵤJᵥ Jᵥ²]: λ₁,λ₂ large = corner; one large = edge; both small = flat.
  • Moravec: min SSD over shifts (fails on diagonal edges).
  • SIFT: DoG D = L(kσ) − L(σ); extrema vs 26 neighbours; orientation histogram; 4×4×8 = 128-D.
  • BRIEF: random pair tests → 256 bits; Hamming; not rot/scale invariant. ORB = oFAST (pyramid, Harris score, intensity-centroid angle) + rBRIEF.
  • FAST: N ≥ 12 contiguous of 16 brighter/darker than I(p) ± t; quick test 1,5,9,13 (≥ 3 of 4).
  • Ratio test d₁/d₂ < θ (strict). Cross-check = mutual NN. Euclid for float, Hamming for binary.
  • Baseline: ORB + Hamming + ratio/cross-check → RANSAC. SIFT for big scale/viewpoint change.
  • SuperGlue: self + cross attention GNN (9 layers) → score matrix + dustbin → Sinkhorn (OT). AUC@20 36.4 → 51.8, precision 50.4 → 84.4%. LightGlue: adaptive depth (early exit) + width (prune points); 44 ms vs 70 ms (adaptive 31 ms).

W3 Camera & stereo

  • x = fX/Z; u = f·X/Z + u₀ (f in px = f_mm/pixel size). K = [f 0 u₀; 0 f v₀; 0 0 1].
  • p̃ = K[R|t]P; C 3×4, scale-free → 11 DOF. Pose (R,t) of camera → extrinsic is its inverse.
  • Lost: depth, lengths, angles, parallelism. Kept: straight lines. Vanishing points/lines.
  • Thin lens 1/f = 1/Z + 1/e; blur R = Lδ/2e; Z ≫ f → pinhole.
  • Bayer RGGB (2× green). Global vs rolling shutter (skew).
  • Z = fb/d, X = Z(u−u₀)/f, Y = Z(v−v₀)/f; δZ ≈ Z²δd/(fb); Zmin = fb/d_max.
  • Big baseline: ↓δZ, ↑Zmin, harder close search. Rectify → search same row.
  • Plane → homography H (8 DOF, 4 pts, DLT + SVD).

W4 Epipolar & RANSAC

  • x′ᵀFx = 0, l′ = Fx; F rank 2, 7 DOF; 8-pt: Af = 0, SVD, force rank 2, normalise.
  • Epipole = other camera centre’s image; all epipolar lines pass through it.
  • Degenerate: pure rotation, planar scene, collinear points; near: small baseline, far points, forward motion.

  • Raising threshold: old inliers stay, outliers may join.
  • Variants: PROSAC, LO-RANSAC, MAGSAC++, GC-, USAC.

W5 Visual odometry

  • Scale ambiguity: K(R·sX + s·t) = s·K(RX + t). Fix with known 3D: s* = argmin Σ‖x − π(K(Rp + st))‖².
  • PnP: 1 pt ∞, 2 pts ∞ (bounded), P3P ≤ 4 solutions (+1 pt), ≥ 4 EPnP. Pipeline: P3P + RANSAC → LM refine on inliers.
  • Residual = obs − pred; inlier if ‖r‖ < τ (norm, not squared).
  • 3D–3D (Arun): centroids; H = Σ x̄ₜ₋₁x̄ₜᵀ; USVᵀ = svd(H); R = VUᵀ; t = x̄ₜ − R x̄ₜ₋₁. Less accurate (stereo depth error).

  • Keyframes: motion threshold, lost features, time; mono: baseline/avg depth > 10–20%.
  • Optimisation template: parameters / prediction / residual / objective. Exact → solve; linear → QR/SVD; nonlinear → GN/LM; outliers → robust/RANSAC.

W6 Graphs & BA

  • Pose graph: X* = argmin Σ‖xᵢ − ⁱTⱼxⱼ‖²_Σ (poses only, relative-pose factors). BA: argmin Σ‖yⱼⁱ − π(pⱼ, xᵢ, K)‖² (poses + landmarks, reprojection).
  • Weight 1/σ²: σ halves → 4× influence.
  • GN: JᵀJΔx = −Jᵀr; LM: (JᵀJ + λI)Δx = −Jᵀr. Sparse normal equations. Needs a good initial guess (local minima).
  • Robust: quadratic (unbounded); L1, Huber k = 1.345 (bounded); Tukey 4.685, Cauchy (redescending); Tukey/Hampel zero influence beyond cut-off.
  • Factor graph: bipartite, undirected; variables + factors. Joint = Π factors.
  • Gaussian fusion: μ = (μ₁/σ₁² + μ₂/σ₂²)/(1/σ₁² + 1/σ₂²), σ² = 1/(Σ1/σᵢ²).
  • MAP + Gaussian = weighted nonlinear LS (log, drop const, Mahalanobis eᵀΣ⁻¹e). MAP loses spread.

W7 SLAM / VIO / eval

  • Direct: photometric error, dense/semi-dense, sub-pixel, needs good init, illumination-sensitive (DSO, DTAM, LSD). Indirect: reprojection, sparse, wide basin (ORB-SLAM, PTAM).
  • IMU: gyro ω + accel a; ã = R(a − g) + b + n; bias → error ½bt² (0.05 m/s², 10 s → 2.5 m). Camera bounds drift; IMU gives scale, robustness, 1–10 kHz.
  • ATE: align (R,T,s), RMSE of positions (global). RPE: relative-motion error (local drift). Report the median too.
  • SLAM = VO + map + loop closure (global consistency). MonoSLAM (EKF), KinectFusion (TSDF), SemanticFusion, SLAM++, QuadricSLAM.
  • ORB-SLAM3: tracking, local mapping (local BA), loop & map merging (DBoW2, pose graph, full BA), Atlas. Relocalise = lost; loop = same map; merge = other map.

W8 Deep learning

  • Task → output / supervision / loss / metric: cls (CE, acc); sem-seg (pixel CE, mIoU); det (cls + box, AP@IoU); inst-seg (mask AP); depth (L2, RMSE); retrieval (contrastive, R@K); obstacle (BCE, P/R at threshold).
  • Backbone → embedding → head. Transfer ladder: feature reuse → linear probe → partial FT → full FT → scratch.
  • Train / val (tune) / test (once). Loss ≠ metric.
  • SSL: contrastive / triplet, MAE, DINO (student–teacher), CLIP.
  • CNN: weight sharing, equivariant (not invariant). ViT: N = HW/P², [class] token, position embeddings, softmax(QKᵀ/√d)V, residual. Less inductive bias → needs big data (ViT-H/14 88.55%).
  • ↓threshold → ↑recall ↑FP; ↑threshold → ↑precision ↑FN. Softmax ≠ calibrated.

W9 Learned 3D

  • Mono depth: ill-posed, global scale ambiguous. Supervision: direct GT / self-sup photometric (I_L(u,v) vs I_R(u−d,v)) / pseudo-labels (Depth Anything V2: synthetic teacher, 62M images).
  • Depth map H×W×1 vs pointmap H×W×3. DUSt3R: pair → 2 pointmaps in cam-1 frame + confidence; up to scale; global alignment (σₑ, Pₑ, Πσ = 1); MASt3R adds descriptors and metric scale.
  • Occ-Net f(x,h) → p_occ; DeepSDF → signed distance; NeRF f(x,y,z,θ,φ) → (σ, RGB), volume rendering, no GT geometry; 3DGS: mean, covariance, opacity, SH colour, splat. LERF/LangSplat + CLIP.
  • Pretty renders ≠ validated occupancy.

W10 Detection

  • Variable output count. Two-stage (anchors 3×3, RPN objectness, top-N, RoI pool, head); one-stage anchor (SSD, RetinaNet) / point (FCOS centerness, YOLO); DETR (queries, Hungarian, no NMS).
  • Losses: CE/BCE/focal + L1/GIoU (+ objectness). Imbalance: focal, sampling, 1-to-1 matching.
  • IoU = I/(A + B − I). TP: IoU ≥ τ, right class, GT not already matched. P = TP/(TP+FP); R = TP/(TP+FN).
  • AP = area under PR (monotone envelope, 101 pts); mAP = mean over classes (COCO also over IoU .5:.95). AP ≠ precision at the deployed threshold.
  • Closed / open set / open vocabulary (CLIP, Grounding DINO); prompts matter; ≠ reasoning.
  • DETR: 42.0 AP = FRCNN-FPN+; APL +7.8, APS −5.5; 100 queries; slow training.

W11 Seg & grasp

  • Semantic (class per pixel) vs instance (mask per object). C logits/pixel; softmax (exclusive) vs sigmoid.
  • Encoder–decoder; pooling / strided conv ↓, unpool / transposed conv ↑; U-Net skips keep boundaries. Mask IoU = |P∩G|/|P∪G|.
  • Imbalance: all-background → high accuracy, obstacle IoU 0.
  • Grasp planar (x,y,θ,w,q); 6-DoF (x,y,z,φ,θ,ψ,w,q). Generative (GG-CNN per pixel, detection-style) vs discriminative (Dex-Net, slow). Diffusion: grasp distribution. Task-aware: language (GraspMolmo, GraspVLA).
  • Contact-GraspNet: contacts in scene cloud, 4-DoF (a, b, w); 17.7M sim grasps; 90.2% vs 80.4%.

W12 Place recognition

  • VPR = image retrieval (not classification): loop closure, kidnapped robot, PnP candidates.
  • Similarity matrix + threshold; single vs multi-session; challenges: viewpoint, illumination, season, dynamics, aliasing.
  • P = TP/(TP+FP); R = TP/GTP; F1 = 2PR/(P+R); max recall @100% P; AUPRC; R@K = share of queries with a correct match in the top K.
  • Global (compact, fast) vs local (robust, costly). BoW + TF-IDF t = (n_id/n_d)·log(N/n_i). NetVLAD; AnyLoc: DINOv2 + GeM/VLAD, domain vocabularies, up to 4× R@1.
  • cos = qᵀa/(‖q‖‖a‖). Triplet / contrastive, hard negatives. Verify geometrically or with sequences (SeqSLAM).

W13 Failure & uncertainty

  • OOD (p_train(x) ≈ 0) vs open set (new class) vs open world (learn new). Covariate p(x); label p(y); concept p(y|x); novel class.
  • Calibrated: n% confidence → n% correct. Bin: acc < conf → over-confident. ECE = Σ(n_b/N)|acc − conf|.
  • Temperature: softmax(z/T), T > 1 softens; argmax unchanged; fit on validation.
  • Epistemic (reducible with data; ensembles, MC dropout) vs aleatoric (irreducible; L = ‖y−ŷ‖²/2σ² + ½ log σ²). Entropy H = −Σp log p.
  • OOD: max softmax, feature distance, energy E = −log Σ e^z. OOD ≠ failure.
  • Selective: predict if r(x) < τ. Coverage = predicted/all; risk = wrong/predicted.
  • Act on risk: abstain, slow down, re-observe, ask a human. No universal threshold.
